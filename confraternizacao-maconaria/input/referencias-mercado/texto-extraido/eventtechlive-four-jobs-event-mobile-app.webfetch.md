# The Four Jobs of an Event Mobile App: A Buyer’s Guide

Adam Parry4 May 2026

0 comments

EVENT TECH LIVE Background

Event Mobile Apps in 2026: What They Have to Do

_The category has matured. The bar is no longer “do you have an app.” It is whether the app does four jobs well enough to justify the download. Most of the market still does two of the four._

---

A well-promoted event app in 2026 reaches between 55% and 65% of registered attendees, according to event app vendor benchmarks compiled across the category, with adoption rising to 80% to 95% only when the organiser eliminates printed alternatives and makes the app the route to check-in (Nunify, 2025; Bizzabo, Q1 2025 Benchmarking Report). Cvent now charges $7 per registrant per event for Attendee Hub plus the Event App, with annual increases starting July 2026 (Cvent, 2025). Eventee publishes a flat $1,499 per event for its Solo tier, $2,999 a year for its Business tier and $4,999 a year for its Enterprise tier (Eventee, 2025). Bizzabo, the largest published competitor of the two, starts at $17,999 a year (Bizzabo, via Software Advice 2025).

In other words, the price-per-attendee for an event app in 2026 spans more than two orders of magnitude. The features being shipped have started to converge. The audience tolerance for downloading another standalone app is finite. The decision an organiser makes in 2026 is less about which app has the most features and more about which app does the four jobs that matter, well enough that the audience uses it on day two.

## **The market context**

Mobile event apps are no longer a contested concept. 91% of event organisers describe apps as essential to event success, and 78% of organisations using a mobile event app report a measurable lift in event ROI (Bizzabo Event Marketing Statistics, 2026 update). The category itself is forecast to grow at an 18% compound annual rate through to the end of the decade (Agorify, 2025). Whether that growth materialises depends on whether the apps shipped in the next three years deliver enough utility to overcome attendee app fatigue.

The adoption picture is more nuanced than the headline figures. According to Nunify’s 2025 benchmarking, organisers who do nothing to promote the app see 20% to 30% adoption. Organisers who promote it well, with exclusive content, gamification, and a pre-event launch, see 55% to 70%. The 80% plus adoption figures only appear when the app is the only path to a printed agenda or to check-in, and even then, accessibility considerations require a non-app fallback (Nunify, 2025). Trainings and workshops hit 64% adoption on average. Conferences and trade shows sit at 61% (Nunify, 2025).

Trends data adds the demand-side picture. More than 40% of attendees say the right technology can make consuming an event much easier (Freeman, 2025). 55% of attendees say the mobile event app can make or break their experience (Bizzabo, citing organiser surveys, 2025). Those are large numbers, but they are conditional. The apps in question have to do their job. Most of the complaints about event apps in 2024 and 2025 had less to do with the existence of an app and more to do with apps that did not load the right agenda, did not store enough data offline, did not pass the accessibility bar, or required attendees to learn a new product for each event they went to.

## **The four jobs of an event app**

Every credible event app on the 2026 market does some combination of four things. The differentiator is which combination, and how well each is done.

### **Job one: agenda and wayfinding utility**

The non-negotiable. The app should give an attendee their personalised agenda, the venue map, the session room they need to be in, push notifications about timing changes, and an offline mode that keeps working when the venue WiFi goes down.

This is the part of the category that has commoditised the fastest. Whova, EventMobi, Guidebook, Eventee, Eventify, CrowdComms, CrowdApps, VenuIQ, Eventogy, Eventscase, Eventbase, SpotMe, and Cvent Attendee Hub all do this at a serviceable level. The differences show up at the edges: how cleanly the app handles agenda changes mid-event, whether speaker bios sync from the registration platform without manual updating, how the venue map performs with venue WiFi degraded, and whether the offline mode is genuine or a fallback to a static agenda.

The questions to ask a vendor on this job are unglamorous and important. What happens to the app if the venue WiFi drops for 30 minutes? Can a session move from Hall A to Hall B at 09:32 and have all 800 affected attendees notified within five minutes? Does the personalised agenda survive a registration platform sync, or do attendees lose their saved sessions when the agenda is republished?

### **Job two: engagement during sessions**

The middle layer. Live polls, Q&A, surveys, audience reaction, gamification, and the various interactivity layers that make a session more than a one-way broadcast.

There is a cluster of specialist platforms that do this well at a session level. Pigeonhole Live, Slido, Mentimeter, Wooclap and Vevox are the often-cited names, and several of them appear in event app vendor stacks as embedded providers rather than competing apps. Within full event app platforms, the engagement story is now more even than it was two years ago. Whova, EventMobi, CrowdComms, VenuIQ, SpotMe, Eventify, Cvent Attendee Hub, and Bizzabo all ship competent in-session polling and Q&A.

The harder question on this job is whether the engagement layer feeds anything useful into the rest of the data flow. A live poll that disappears into a vendor portal at the end of the event has limited value. A live poll that updates a sponsor’s report, refreshes a CRM record for the attendee who voted, and triggers a follow-up email through marketing automation has compounding value. Few platforms have closed that loop end-to-end.

### **Job three: networking and matchmaking**

The space where AI claims have been the loudest and the evidence has been the patchiest. Most enterprise event apps now ship some form of AI-powered attendee matchmaking, AI agenda recommendation, or AI-driven content suggestion.

Cvent’s Attendee Hub Event App is now powered by CventIQ, which the vendor describes as an AI-powered attendee assistant that learns from attendee behaviour and engagement data across the platform (Cvent, 2025). Bizzabo’s AI Companion, branded Bizzy, performs a similar role and feeds into Bizzabo’s AI Sponsor ROI Engine (Bizzabo, 2025). EventMobi, Whova, Eventify, CrowdComms, VenuIQ, SpotMe and the specialist matchmaking platforms Brella, Swapcard, Grip and MatchPoint all describe AI-driven matchmaking in their 2026 product positioning.

The honest position on AI matchmaking, based on vendor-published case studies, is that it lifts meeting acceptance rates by a single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage when implemented well, with substantial variance by event type and audience density. Vendor-sourced numbers in the high double-digits should be treated as case studies, not category benchmarks. The right RFP question is: what is the meeting acceptance rate at a real customer event of similar audience size, with and without AI recommendation. A vendor that cannot answer with numbers from a named event should be probed harder.

### **Job four: data capture and analytics**

The sleeper job and arguably the one with the largest commercial weight in 2026\. Every interaction inside the app, from session attendance to poll vote to sponsor booth visit to one-to-one meeting, is signal. Whether that signal flows back into the organiser’s CRM, into sponsor reports, and into next year’s planning is the question that decides the commercial value of the app.

Cvent’s pitch on Attendee Hub is the clearest articulation of this. AI capabilities facilitate engagement through chat, polls, and surveys, and the data flows back through the broader Cvent platform to inform follow-up activity (Cvent, 2025). Bizzabo’s positioning is structurally similar. Whova, EventMobi, CrowdComms and VenuIQ all ship CRM and marketing automation integrations of varying maturity. The integration story, as with the registration platforms in the same vendor’s stack, is the one buyers should test in the demo with their own CRM connected.

The implicit question on this job is whether the organiser owns the data collected by the app, in what format the data leaves the platform at end of contract, and how granular the consent capture is. Article 20 of the GDPR mandates that personal data should be available in a “structured, commonly used, machine-readable and interoperable format” (GDPR Article 20). The same standard should apply to the organiser’s right to extract its own behavioural data from an app at end of contract.

## **The vendor landscape**

The 2026 event app market is best read as four overlapping clusters. The clusters are defined more by audience and pricing model than by feature set, because the feature set has converged.

### **Enterprise event-platform apps**

The apps that ship as part of a larger enterprise event platform, where the app itself is rarely the lead reason for the purchase but inherits the ecosystem features.

Cvent Attendee Hub is the largest by registrant volume. The pricing model, $7 per registrant per event, makes the cost structure unusually transparent for an enterprise product (Cvent, 2025). Bizzabo’s Event Experience OS includes a native app, with engagement tied to its Klik onsite wearable for clients that have bought into that stack. RainFocus, Stova, vFairs, Webex Events, and Zoom Events all sit in this cluster.

### **Specialist event app platforms**

The platforms whose primary product is the app itself, often with a registration layer alongside.

Whova has a strong position with professional conferences and academic associations, and is consistently named in the comparison shortlists. EventMobi, Canadian-headquartered, has been in market since 2009 with named clients including Visa and TD Bank, and competes on transparent pricing and a registration-and-app pairing (EventMobi, 2025). Guidebook has historically led on the simplest organiser experience and now publishes an unlimited-app pricing option with no per-attendee fee (Guidebook, 2025). Eventify is built around the assumption that exhibitors and sponsors deserve as much app-side attention as attendees, with a B2B trade show focus. SpotMe sits at the high end of this cluster with a long enterprise-conference history. CrowdComms is well established with UK association and corporate event teams and bundles registration, app, networking, gamification and virtual delivery with managed service. CrowdApps, VenuIQ, Eventogy, Eventscase, Eventbase, Fliplet and Eventee occupy adjacent positions in the same cluster, with VenuIQ in particular pushing onsite delegate tracking as a differentiator (VenuIQ, 2025).

### **Networking and matchmaking specialists**

Platforms whose primary product is the networking and meeting layer, often integrated into a broader app stack rather than replacing it.

Brella, Swapcard, Grip, MatchPoint and InEvent sit in this cluster. The case for these platforms over a one-stop event app has been the depth of the matchmaking algorithm and the proven mechanics of meeting scheduling at large B2B events. The case against, in 2026, is that the in-house AI matchmaking inside the larger event app platforms has narrowed the gap.

### **Engagement-only specialists**

The narrower cluster of platforms that focus exclusively on the in-session engagement layer.

Pigeonhole Live, Slido, Mentimeter, Wooclap and Vevox sit in this cluster. The economics here favour shorter-cycle deployment and integration with whatever event app the organiser has already chosen. For organisations running a portfolio of small-to-medium events with high audience interactivity needs, an engagement specialist plus a lightweight app may be a more sensible stack than a single enterprise platform.

## **A practical RFP framework**

Five questions that should be answered in writing before any pricing conversation.

### **One: who owns the data the app collects**

The contract should be unambiguous. The organiser owns all event behavioural data captured through the app. The vendor is a data processor under GDPR. Granular consent, including consent for individual data uses such as sharing with a sponsor, should be tracked in a way that survives an export.

### **Two: what does the integration with your registration platform actually do**

The minimum bar is that registration data flows into the app at registration time and behavioural data flows back out at the end of each day. The next question is whether the data flow is bidirectional in real time. The next question after that is whether session attendance, sponsor booth visits, and one-to-one meetings update the relevant CRM and sponsor reports automatically.

A 2026 event app that does not integrate cleanly with the registration platform feeding it is a procurement mistake, not a tooling problem. The integration tests should run before the design conversation.

### **Three: what is the offline experience**

For any event held in a venue with patchy or shared WiFi, the offline experience is not a nice-to-have. The personalised agenda, the venue map, the session details and the contact list should remain available offline. Push notifications should queue and deliver when connectivity returns. The questions to ask are how much data is cached, how it is refreshed, and what happens to a poll vote cast offline.

### **Four: what is the accessibility position**

By 2026, the baseline expectation is WCAG 2.2 AA compliance for every screen of the app, including all attendee-facing screens and the in-app forms (Continual Engine, 2025). AI-driven real-time captioning is increasingly a default feature. CRM-linked storage of attendee accommodation preferences is a maturity indicator. Any vendor unwilling to publish a Voluntary Product Accessibility Template, or its UK equivalent, should explain why.

### **Five: what does the data exit look like**

The same question that applies to a registration platform applies to an event app. At end of contract, in what format does the organiser get its event data, on what timeline, at what cost? The right answer is “machine-readable, within 30 days, no additional cost.” Anything else is a future negotiation problem.

## **Future outlook: where event apps go next**

Three trends to track between now and 2028.

First, the fold-in. The standalone event app, in its current form, will not survive the next three years for many B2B events. Cvent’s CventIQ-driven Attendee Hub model, where the app is the surfacing layer for the platform’s data, is an early version of where the category is heading. Expect more enterprise platforms to deemphasise the standalone app brand in favour of an embedded experience that connects across registration, the app, the onsite wearable, and the post-event content. Specialist app platforms will respond by either deepening their registration-and-onsite story, as Whova, EventMobi and Bizzabo have already done, or by leaning hard into a specific niche.

Second, the agentic shift. The early-stage signal from Cvent, Bizzabo and Swapcard is that AI assistants embedded in the event app will move from passive recommendation to active agency. An attendee could ask their app to schedule a meeting with three relevant vendors at lunch on day two and have the meetings booked, the diaries reconciled and the venue rooms reserved without the attendee touching any other tool. None of this is shipping at scale in 2026, but the building blocks are in production.

Third, the consent and accessibility floor. The combination of EU AI Act transparency requirements, UK GDPR enforcement, the EU Accessibility Act, and the operational expectations of corporate clients running large internal events will push the floor on consent management, accessibility, and data portability higher in every RFP. Apps that are weak on any of those three points will struggle to win against apps that are not.

A fourth, more speculative trend is the resurgence of single-purpose engagement specialists embedded into other apps. Slido inside a Cvent app, Pigeonhole Live inside a CrowdComms app. The architecture has been technically possible for years and the partner mechanics are getting smoother. Whether organisers want a more componentised stack or a more bundled one is the harder question.

## **Wrap and next steps**

Three actions for an organiser running an event app RFP in 2026.

Define the four jobs in your event’s terms before you take the first vendor demo. What does utility mean for your venue and your audience. What does engagement mean for your session formats. What does networking mean for your attendee mix. What data do you actually want back. Most vendors can demo most things. The shortlist comes from which vendor demos most usefully against your event.

Test the registration-to-app data flow in the demo with a real CRM. Anything that cannot be tested in a demo will be a problem in production.

Insist on accessibility documentation and a written exit clause. Both will be standard within two years and easier to negotiate now than later.

Most of the apps named in this article are competent at the first job, the agenda and wayfinding utility. Fewer are competent at all four. The right app is the one that does the four jobs your event needs, integrates cleanly into the rest of your stack, and gives you the data back when you walk away. Everything else is decoration.
