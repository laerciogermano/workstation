How to outline a nonfiction book: five structures and a chapter template

Pick the structure that matches what your reader needs, list your chapters in that order with a one-sentence point for each, then outline every chapter with a hook, a thesis and its supporting points. Most nonfiction books fit one of five structures, shown below with sample tables of contents.

By Scribe MediaPublished October 20, 20268 minute read

Why the outline matters more than the draft

A good outline does three jobs. It proves the book has enough in it to be a book. It shows you, before you write, which chapters you are weakest on. And it means you never sit down to a blank page: every writing session starts with a chapter you have already planned.

You need two things from earlier stages: the reader profile from Stage 1 and the one-sentence promise from Stage 2. The structure you pick should be the shortest path from the reader's starting point to that promise.

Step 1: Get every chapter idea out of your head

Before choosing a structure, dump everything. Write each idea, story, framework, mistake and question on its own line or sticky note. Three prompts help:

  • The workshop prompt. If you had to teach this as a one-day workshop, what would the sessions be?
  • The client prompt. What do you tell every new client in the first three meetings?
  • The question prompt. What are the ten questions readers in your profile ask most?

Keep a "parking lot" for ideas that do not fit. The Scribe Method's outline chapter recommends exactly this: some of those ideas find a home later, and some become your next book.

Step 2: Choose a structure

Here are five structures that cover most professional nonfiction. Each sample table of contents is an example we wrote, not a real book.

1. Steps or framework

Use it when the reader needs to do something in order, and you have a method you teach the same way each time. Watch out for chapters that are too uneven: one step that takes a page next to another that needs 8,000 words.

Sample: a book for owners preparing to sell a family business

  1. Introduction: The sale starts two years before the sale
  2. Step 1: Decide what you actually want from the exit
  3. Step 2: Get your numbers buyer-ready
  4. Step 3: Make yourself unnecessary
  5. Step 4: Build the team (lawyer, banker, accountant)
  6. Step 5: Run a quiet market test
  7. Step 6: Negotiate terms, not just price
  8. Step 7: Plan your first year after
  9. Conclusion: The owner who sold well

2. Problem, then solution

Use it when readers do not yet agree they have the problem, or misunderstand its cause. The first third persuades; the rest teaches. Watch out for a problem section so long that readers quit before the help arrives.

Sample: a book for hospital administrators about nurse turnover

  1. Introduction: The hidden cost on every unit
  2. Part 1, the problem: Why nurses really leave
  3. Why raises and bonuses have not worked
  4. What the exit interviews miss
  5. Part 2, the solution: The charge nurse is the job
  6. Scheduling as a retention tool
  7. The first 90 days on the unit
  8. Measuring what matters
  9. Conclusion: One unit, one year

3. Story-led

Use it when your experience is the proof, and readers will learn best by living through it with you. Each chapter is a period or event, and each ends with the lesson it taught. Watch out for stories that serve you more than the reader. Keep only the parts that teach.

Sample: a founder's book on surviving a near-bankruptcy

  1. Introduction: Ninety days of cash
  2. The year everything worked (and why that was the problem)
  3. The call from the bank
  4. Telling the team the truth
  5. The customers who stayed
  6. Cutting without cutting the future
  7. The turnaround nobody noticed
  8. What I run differently now
  9. Conclusion: Your ninety days

4. Principles or rules

Use it when your expertise is a set of beliefs or habits that apply in many situations, rather than a sequence. Each chapter stands alone, so readers can dip in. Watch out for principles that overlap. If two chapters could swap examples, merge them.

Sample: a book for new partners at professional services firms

  1. Introduction: Nobody trains you to be a partner
  2. Rule 1: Your calendar is your strategy
  3. Rule 2: Sell to people you already serve
  4. Rule 3: Say the hard thing in the first meeting
  5. Rule 4: Hire for the client you want next
  6. Rule 5: Price the outcome, not the hours
  7. Rule 6: Leave before you are tired
  8. Conclusion: The partner you meant to be

5. Questions

Use it when your readers arrive with predictable questions, often in a stressful moment, and want answers fast. Each chapter title is a question in the reader's words. Watch out for a book that reads like an FAQ page. Order the questions as the reader would meet them, and tell a story in each answer.

Sample: a book for adult children managing a parent's care

  1. Introduction: The phone call you did not expect
  2. How do I know it is time?
  3. Who decides, and how do we talk about it?
  4. Can we afford this?
  5. Home care, assisted living or memory care?
  6. What paperwork do we need now?
  7. How do I take care of myself too?
  8. Conclusion: The questions get easier

Can I mix structures? Yes. A common blend is problem then solution for the overall shape, with the solution chapters laid out as steps. Pick one structure for the table of contents so the reader can see the path; blend inside chapters if you need to.

Step 3: Set your table of contents

Put your chapters in order and give each one a one or two sentence thesis: the single point the reader should take away. The Scribe Method suggests 5 to 15 chapters as a good range for most books. Test the order by reading only the theses from top to bottom. They should read like a short, convincing argument.

  • If two theses say nearly the same thing, merge the chapters.
  • If a thesis needs "and," it may be two chapters.
  • If a chapter has no clear thesis, it may belong in the parking lot.

Step 4: Outline each chapter (the chapter outline template)

Now give every chapter the same skeleton. This is the book outline template we recommend. Keep it to bullet points.

Part of the chapter outlineWhat to writeExample (from the exit book above)
Working titleA plain label. You will polish titles later.Make yourself unnecessary
HookThe story, fact or question that opens the chapter.A buyer walks away when the owner admits every big client calls him directly.
ThesisThe one point, in one or two sentences.A business that depends on its owner is worth less, so the owner must step back before the sale.
Supporting pointsThree to five points, in order, each with its proof.Why buyers discount owner-dependent firms; the three dependencies to fix; how to hand off clients.
Stories and evidenceWhich stories, client cases or data go where.Two client examples; one of the author's own mistakes.
Reader actionWhat the reader does after the chapter.List every task only you can do, then pick three to hand off this quarter.
Open questionsWhat you still need to find out or check.Need permission from the client in story two.

The "open questions" line is easy to skip and worth keeping. It turns into your research list for Stage 4.

Copy this chapter outline template into your document once per chapter:

CHAPTER [number]: [working title]

Hook: [the story, fact or question that opens the chapter]
Thesis: [the one point, in one or two sentences]
Supporting points:
  1. [point] / proof: [story, client case or sourced fact]
  2. [point] / proof: [...]
  3. [point] / proof: [...]
Reader action: [what the reader does after this chapter]
Open questions: [what you still need to find out, check or get permission for]

Or fill it in on screen with the outline builder, which keeps every chapter's point, story and takeaway in one place.

The whole nonfiction book outline template

The chapter template covers one chapter. This one holds the whole book on a page or two, so you can see the argument from start to finish before you write. Fill in the top section first: if you cannot say who the book is for and what it promises, the chapters will drift.

Copy this nonfiction book outline template into a new document:

BOOK: [working title]
Reader: [one specific person, in one sentence]
Promise: [what the reader can do or understand by the last page]
Structure: [one of the five structures above]

INTRODUCTION
  The problem: [what the reader is struggling with, in their words]
  The promise: [what this book will do about it]
  Why you: [the experience that lets you write it]
  How to use the book: [read in order, or jump to the part you need]

PART ONE: [name, if you use parts]
  Chapter 1: [working title] / thesis: [one point]
  Chapter 2: [working title] / thesis: [one point]
  Chapter 3: [working title] / thesis: [one point]

PART TWO: [name]
  Chapter 4: [working title] / thesis: [one point]
  Chapter 5: [working title] / thesis: [one point]
  [add chapters until the argument is complete]

CONCLUSION
  What the reader now knows: [the theses, in a few lines]
  What to do next: [the first step after closing the book]

BACK MATTER
  [resources, worksheets, notes, about the author]

PARKING LOT
  [ideas that do not fit yet]

Then give every chapter line its own chapter outline from Step 4. The outline builder does both on screen and lets you download the result.

How to know the outline is done

  • Every chapter has a thesis, a hook and at least three supporting points.
  • Reading the theses in order makes a clear case for your one-sentence promise.
  • Nothing in the book is there only because you find it interesting.
  • Your weakest chapter is obvious, and you have a plan for its open questions.
  • You could hand the outline to a colleague and they could explain the book back to you.

Common questions

Is there a chapter outline template I can copy?

Yes. Step 4 on this page has a plain-text chapter outline template: working title, hook, thesis, three to five supporting points with their proof, the reader action and your open questions. Copy it once per chapter, or use the free outline builder on this site.

How many chapters should a nonfiction book have?

The Scribe Method suggests 5 to 15 chapters as a good range for most books, and notes that most nonfiction books land between 5 and 20. Fewer than five usually means chapters are carrying too many ideas; more than 20 can mean they are too thin.

How detailed should a book outline be?

Detailed enough that you never sit down to a blank chapter: a working title, a hook, a one or two sentence thesis, and the supporting points in order. Bullet points, not paragraphs. If you are writing full sentences in the outline, you have started drafting.

Can I change my outline once I start writing?

Yes, and you probably will. Treat the outline as a plan, not a contract. If a chapter refuses to work, check it against your one-sentence promise and reader profile before you change the structure, then update the outline so it stays accurate.

Is there a free book outline template?

Yes. This guide's free outline builder walks you through the structure and every chapter. Your outline is saved in your own browser, not sent to us.

Do I need parts as well as chapters?

Only if you have many chapters that fall into clear groups. Parts help a reader see the shape of a 15 to 20 chapter book. Most books under 12 chapters do not need them.
